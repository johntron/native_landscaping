Start here · Six letters, one yard

PLANTS: what a Blackland Prairie yard needs, in order

An acronym is not evidence — it is a way to hold six separately-sourced pieces of advice in your head at once. Two of these are things you plant; the rest are things you do once something is in the ground. Where the site can already check a letter against your own yard, the card says where.

P

Plant natives that fit your site

Not just native — native to a spot with your light, soil and drainage. Wasting water on a species that will not thrive here helps nobody. Check a yard against this, or see which genera clear the flora screen below.

L

Leave the leaves, stems, and seed heads

Leaf litter, standing stems and winter seed heads are overwinter habitat for cavity- and stem-nesting bees, not mess. Texas Property Code §202.007(a)(1) protects composting vegetation and grass clippings — an HOA cannot ban this outright.

A

Avoid pesticides

Broad-spectrum insecticides do not distinguish the caterpillar you planted for from anything else eating a leaf. Spot-treat a named problem; do not spray on a schedule.

N

No invasives

Some of what garden centers sell here escapes cultivation and displaces the natives above. A confirmed avoid-list for this area exists; it is not yet linked from here.

T

Trees first, especially native oaks

Canopy is the layer a yard is slowest to grow back and the one the keystone genera below lean on hardest — Quercus is the single strongest card in the comparison below. Check a yard for which vertical layers it is missing.

S

Something blooming or fruiting every season

A single spring flush leaves pollinators and birds with nothing the rest of the year. Check a yard for gaps in bloom and fruit continuity across the seasons.

Two things you do, not plant. These left the acronym but not the site — they are the best-evidenced items here, just not things that grow. Loss, Will & Marra (2013, Nature Communications 4:1396) estimate free-ranging cats kill 1.3–4.0 billion birds a year in the US, very likely the single largest human-linked cause of bird death; on that aggregate, one outdoor housecat plausibly does more damage to birds than everything else on this page put together — that per-yard read is my arithmetic, not the paper's headline number. Loss, Loss, Will & Marra (2014, The Condor 116(1)) put building-collision deaths at 365–988 million a year, 44% of it at houses one to three stories tall — the plainest fix there is marking glass the birds can't tell is there. Keep the cat indoors first; it outweighs the window.

What belongs here · and what eats it

Where the continental keystone list and the regional flora disagree

The keystone-genera advice most native-plant groups hand out is the printed National Wildlife Federation Level I ecoregion-9 list — a unit that runs from Texas to the Canadian aspen parkland — so it recommends larch, hemlock, fir and spruce for a Blackland Prairie yard. NWF's own zip-code Native Plant Finder tool is more precise: it filters to a plant's historic county-level range and would not return those genera for a Dallas ZIP. But the printed list is still the one that circulates, and it is what most people mean by "keystone genera." Below, every genus on it is put to the flora: does it have a treatment in FNCT, does the flora's own host-plant appendix name Lepidoptera for it, and are any of those animals actually recorded near this site? Where the two sources disagree, the flora wins — and the rejects stay visible rather than being quietly dropped.

Part three · The ask

What I need from this room

I am not proposing a new organization. NPSOT, BPTMN, Pollinator Pathway and Homegrown National Park already exist and already do the organizing. I am asking three questions.

ASK 01

Red-line the screen

Every row carries an FNCT page number and, where I overrode a source, a sentence saying the call was mine. Those are where I am least sure. I would rather be corrected here than in front of a city arborist.

ASK 02

Does a chapter yard registry already exist?

If members have been logging conversions — NLCP graduates, Wildscapes certifications, anything — that list is worth more than any model I can build. The targeting argument is only useful with real points on it.

ASK 03

Is there appetite for a coordinated effort at all?

Part one argues that adjacency beats linear connection. That is untested here. Before anyone builds a participant-facing anything, I would like to know whether this chapter thinks the question is worth answering.

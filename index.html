Your yard could be an oasis — or a desert

To a caterpillar or a hungry chickadee, a lawn of turf grass and imported shrubs might as well be sand — nothing there to eat, nowhere to nest. Six habits turn it back into an oasis. Meet PLANTS — a memory aid I made up, not a study. The table below is the part that's rigorously sourced.

P

Plant natives that fit your yard

Right plant, right spot. Check your yard or browse what qualifies.

L

Leave the leaves

Fallen leaves and old stems are where bees spend the winter. Texas law has your back if an HOA disagrees.

A

Avoid pesticides

Bugs are the whole point. Broad sprays kill the ones you were rooting for too.

N

No invasives

A few yard favorites turn into neighborhood problems. Skip these:

    One oak can feed hundreds of caterpillar species. Nothing else here comes close. Check your yard.

    Keystone genera, checked against the local flora

    What belongs here

    A handful of plant genera do most of the ecological work in a yard — the "keystone genera" that feed the most caterpillars and specialist bees. The usual list for them is quoted at a scale the size of Texas-to-Canada, so below, every genus is checked against the region's own flora instead. Some don't hold up — those stay visible, marked as rejected, rather than being quietly dropped.

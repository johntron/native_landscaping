Blackland Patch Network

Two questions a yard-by-yard habitat effort has to answer before it recruits anybody: where the patches go, and what goes in them.

What this is and isn't. Part one is a schematic — invented geometry demonstrating a threshold argument, not survey data and not a site plan. Part two is real: every genus is screened against Shinners & Mahler's Illustrated Flora of North Central Texas (Diggs, Lipscomb & O'Kennon 1999) with the page cited, and every larval host is from that flora's own Appendix Ten. Where I have made a judgement call rather than quoted a source, the row says so and says it is mine. I expect some of it to be wrong. Telling me which parts is the entire point of showing it to you.

Part one · Geometry (schematic)

The same number of yards, arranged two ways

Most yard-habitat programs accept whoever signs up, so the resulting pattern is effectively random. But the scale that matters for an insectivorous bird is a territory — one to two hectares, several adjacent lots. Each hexagon below is roughly that size. Move the slider, then flip the arrangement.

The assumption I'd most like challenged. Narango, Tallamy & Marra (2018, PNAS) found Carolina chickadee populations fail below roughly 70% native plant biomass within a territory. I am treating "40% of lots converted" as a stand-in for that. Those are not the same measurement and the conversion between them is a guess. The geometric point survives a wrong threshold; any specific number here does not. Redundancy also only buffers uncorrelated risk — drought, a hard freeze, citywide mosquito fogging and owner turnover hit every yard in a cluster at once.

Part two · What goes in them

Where the continental keystone list and the regional flora disagree

The standard advice is to plant "keystone genera" from the National Wildlife Federation's list, which is keyed to EPA Level I ecoregion 9 — a unit that runs from Texas to the Canadian aspen parkland. It therefore recommends larch, hemlock, fir and spruce for a Blackland Prairie yard. Below, every genus is put to the flora: does it have a treatment in FNCT, does the flora's own host-plant appendix name Lepidoptera for it, and are any of those animals actually recorded near this site? Where the two sources disagree, the flora wins — and the rejects stay visible rather than being quietly dropped.

Part three · The ask

What I need from this room

I am not proposing a new organization. NPSOT, BPTMN, Pollinator Pathway and Homegrown National Park already exist and already do the organizing. I am asking three questions.

ASK 01

Red-line the screen

Every row carries an FNCT page number and, where I overrode a source, a sentence saying the call was mine. Those are where I am least sure. I would rather be corrected here than in front of a city arborist.

ASK 02

Does a chapter yard registry already exist?

If members have been logging conversions — NLCP graduates, Wildscapes certifications, anything — that list is worth more than any model I can build. The targeting argument is only useful with real points on it.

ASK 03

Is there appetite for a coordinated effort at all?

Part one argues that adjacency beats linear connection. That is untested here. Before anyone builds a participant-facing anything, I would like to know whether this chapter thinks the question is worth answering.
